Reading-Don Norman, Design of Everyday Things

Source

Bad everyday design: doors, switches, or sinks that confuse users. Good design = discoverability (what can I do?) + understanding (what does it mean?).

Don’t blame users: errors often come from poor design, not “human error.” Systems should guide recovery when things go wrong.

Five key principles:

1. Affordances → possible actions.

2. Signifiers → clues on how to act.

3. Mapping → intuitive link between control and result.

4. Feedback → clear, timely response to actions.

5. Conceptual models → mental picture of how things work.

Affordances vs. signifiers: affordance = what’s possible, signifier = what tells you it’s possible. Designers often confuse them.

Technology paradox: more features make products powerful but often harder to use (e.g., digital watches).

Design challenge: balance usability, functionality, cost, and aesthetics. Human-centered design helps make products people actually enjoy.

Add a Digital Input

Pull-down resistor:It is used in the input that has the possibility of floating, like a switch or a pushbutton. Its job is to keep the pin at a stable LOW (0V) when it isn't actively driven HIGH. Without it, the signal would be floating.

A floating input is a pin that's connected to neither Vcc (HIGH) nor GND (LOW). It's just left hanging. In this state, the pin behaves like a tiny capacitor—extremely sensitive to the environment:

Your hand nearby might induce a charge, making the voltage jump.

Electromagnetic noise could flip the reading between HIGH and LOW at random.

Result: in your code, digitalRead() returns unpredictable values—sometimes HIGH, sometimes LOW.

In short: a pull-down resistor makes sure the “switch” defaults to OFF, and a value around 10 kΩ usually does the job.
