When Laughter Turns into Unease: Reflections on Yes Men, 4 July 2009 New York Times, 2009

At first glance, it was hilarious. Ordinary people picked up the paper, glanced at the headlines, and then looked again in confusion. Their faces shifted from surprise to puzzlement, sometimes even blank stares. I couldn’t help laughing. The absurdity was so palpable that it sparked a genuine, joyful sense of humor in me. Rather than shouting slogans or lecturing audiences, they construct situations that are almost believable, yet slightly too good to be true. That moment of cognitive dissonance makes people laugh, and laughter opens the door to reflection.

And yet, there is another side. To be deceived, even for just a moment, can feel manipulative. You are invited to believe in a hopeful vision, only to have it ripped away. That emotional swing—from joy to sadness—leaves behind a sense of betrayal.

More troublingly, the project risks trivializing the suffering of those who were actually in Iraq at the time. For New Yorkers, the fake paper was a moment of comic shock, a political prank, an art experiment. But for people living under bombs, displacement, and death, the war was not a metaphor or an artistic medium—it was their daily reality. Watching a newspaper announce “Iraq War Ends” while the war raged on could easily feel disrespectful, reducing their pain to a rhetorical device for Western audiences.

Writing this reflection reminded me that my own emotions—whether laughter, discomfort, or anger—are not only valid but essential in engaging with art. Trusting these instincts allows me to encounter works like the Yes Men’s newspaper not just as clever interventions, but as experiences that move me, unsettle me, and ultimately deepen my understanding of what art can do.
