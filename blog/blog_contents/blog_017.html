Outline of my Experimental Short-Untitled

Genre: Horror-supernatural horror/mockumentary

Duration: 3 minutes

Time: Early 2000s

Place: China/Japan/America

The Core Premise

Somewhere in a university film club, a dozen students began to have collectively developed insomnia, claiming that when they closed their eyes, they could vaguely see a blurred human face.

As they contacted a television program for help, an investigation began. Through interviews and research, it was found that these students all attended the same movie screening organized by the club. The movie is called Cicada—an unnoticed relic, the kind of small philosophical love story that only a handful of cinephiles ever watched.

After meticulously examining the videotapes used for the screening, investigators discovered a single frame that doesn’t belong to the movie—the exact face described by the students—gender unknown, age unknown, identity unknown. No one knows who inserted it, or when. The frame itself became a portal. Viewers who saw it can’t unsee it. When they closed their eyes, the afterimage remained—an imprint burned into the retina, playing endlessly behind their eyelids.

Media Horror

This story sits in the lineage of media horror: fear born not from monsters but from the medium itself. A forgotten film becomes a haunted artifact—its corruption hiding in the smallest visual detail, like a historical wound encoded in light. That single wrong frame suggests the return of what has been erased—a lost collective trauma, or the residue of art endlessly copied until its spirit decays. In an era flooded with images, only error and anomaly regain a sense of reality. The curse is not supernatural—it’s technological, archival, cultural.

Watching the Act of Watching

The narrative folds back on itself:

·The students watch the film.

·The documentary team watches the students’ footage.

·The audience watches the documentary—and therefore, the cursed frame.

Each layer mirrors the next until the boundaries collapse. That frame becomes the origin of all gazes—the point where seeing and being seen exchange places.

The Slow Voice of Time

My film ends with that frame replayed in slow motion—its distorted time stretching the human voice of the movie Cicada into something unbearable. A slowed human voice exposes the flesh of time—it ceases to be human speech and becomes the vibration of duration itself. The viewer no longer sees an image but hears time scream softly as it’s stretched beyond recognition. That single frame is not an image anymore; it is time devouring itself.

The Afterimage and the Body as Screen

The most unsettling mutation comes when the frame escapes the film. Anyone who has seen it begins to suffer from insomnia. Closing the eyes no longer grants darkness; the cursed image flickers faintly inside, like a phantom projected onto the mind. The eye becomes another screen. The film invades physiology—vision becomes infection. It’s a form of existential horror, not about death, but about the collapse of inside and outside, viewer and viewed, body and media.

The Empty Space

The questions of who inserted the frame and whose face is that frame are never answered. That blank is crucial. It’s the void that allows the horror to grow inside the audience’s own imagination.

Closing Note

This concept isn’t just about a haunted film. It’s about what happens when the act of seeing itself becomes haunted—when images stop reflecting the world and begin to reproduce our nightmares. A forgotten reel, a wrong frame, a sleepless eye: together they form a quiet, creeping meditation on what it means to live in a world where every light leaves a scar.

Reading-On the Rights of Molotov Man

Source

Reading On the Rights of Molotov Man reminds me of Hiroki Azuma’s Otaku: Japanese Animal Database and Baudrillard’s Simulacra and Simulation. I can’t help but see a common thread: the added value of simulation.

The Shared Theme: The Growth of “the Simulated”

Azuma looks at otaku culture and notices that fans no longer consume grand narratives, but instead pick out isolated “moe elements” from a database. Cat ears, glasses, or tsundere traits can circulate across countless works. Originality becomes blurry; what matters is the element itself.

Baudrillard argues that in contemporary society, symbols detach from reality. The more “pure” they become, the less they need to reference anything real. We end up in a system of signs that refer only to each other, creating what he calls hyperreality.

The Molotov Man debate plays out on exactly this fault line. Joy Garnett treats the image as an aesthetic fragment, while Susan Meiselas insists on restoring the rebel Pablo Arauz to his specific history and struggle. Their clash is about whether images belong to a free-floating network of signs or to concrete human contexts.

Meiselas tries to protect Pablo’s individuality—“a person, in a place, at a moment.” Garnett abstracts him into a larger-than-life gesture, a fragment that can be endlessly reused. In other words, Arauz shifts from being a man in history to a kind of “database element,” a pose that can circulate across contexts.

My Own Unease

I admit I feel torn. On one hand, Meiselas’s position feels almost heroic: swimming against the current of decontextualization, insisting on giving Arauz his dignity back. On the other hand, Garnett’s stance clearly reflects the spirit of our time, where images inevitably escape their origins and float in global circulation. This contradiction is precisely what troubles me: De-contextualization gives images freedom and added value, but risks disrespect and flattening while re-contextualization preserves human weight, but struggles to resist the tidal wave of appropriation in the digital age.

Closing Note

Azuma, Baudrillard, and the Molotov Man debate are three mirrors reflecting the same condition:

Database consumption (Azuma)

Hyperreal systems of signs (Baudrillard)

The fate of images in circulation (Meiselas vs. Garnett)

My own response sits uneasily in the middle: I’m fascinated by the power of simulation, but I also mourn the loss of respect and specificity. Maybe this unease is the truest symptom of our postmodern moment—caught between the allure of free-floating signs and the longing to hold onto what is real.
