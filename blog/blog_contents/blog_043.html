1-Reaction to the COVID Tracking Project Podcast

"Covid 19 has been the great equalizer."

That's not true. But what stands out most was not that it is wrong, but how easily it spread—and how badly we seemed to need it. The idea didn't emerge from data or careful observation; it emerged from absence. When racial data were missing, equality became an assumption rather than a claim that needed proof. Under such circumstances, a poetic sentence spoken from a bathtub by a celebrity could harden into political truth. What the podcast makes painfully clear is that this myth did work. It softened fear. It reassured those with privilege that vulnerability was evenly distributed, that no special responsibility was required. But once the data began to surface—once Black, Native, and Latinx communities appeared disproportionately in death counts—the equalizer narrative collapsed almost instantly. The virus had not changed; visibility had. This raises a deeper question: how often do we mistake ignorance for fairness?

2-Missing Data

My reaction towards the podcast happen to align perfectly with the central theme of The Library of Missing Datasets by Mimi Onuoha

2.1-What datasets do you think can possibly be categorized as “missing data”

The death toll of the "Arduous March" (1990s North Korean famine)

2.2-Make a list and provide evidence that this data is missing, or present a rationale for why you think it might be missing.

In the literature I've consulted online, e.g. The Demographic Impacts of Prolonged Famine in North Korea by Kim, Hyungseog, this dataset appears only as an estimate and never as a precise value.

Regarding the “Arduous March”: This was a nationwide catastrophe that lasted for years. It involved the deaths of hundreds of thousands to millions of people. By modern state governance logic, there should have been death registrations, population changes, and nutritional data. Yet all such data remain in a state of obscurity. This aligns with Mimi Onuoha's definition of missing data: data that should exist but in fact does not.

Its absence is not accidental but structural. The state actively manufactures “invisibility.” The North Korean government withholds accurate population data, prohibits independent investigations, and politicizes and militarizes narratives of famine. Thus death is: completely erased from public discourse, constituting an active absence.
