My Community

1. 50+ years old

2. living in southern China

3. consider themselves to have belief in Guanyin

More specifically, the five respondents share the following profile: all are female; all began Guanyin worship in adulthood rather than childhood (ranging from late twenties to mid-forties); and all are first-generation or early-generation smartphone users with limited or no online religious engagement.

Questions

2. Do you think Guanyin hears you? What do you usually say to her?

3. Have you witnessed anyone blessed through Guanyin worship? Is it prayer-dependent or random?

4. Compared to your generation, do you think young people today still believe in these things? What are your thoughts on this?

5. Do you use your phone regularly? What do you use it for? If yes, have you engaged in any Guanyin-related activities online?

6. Have you heard of using phones/AI for fortune-telling? How does online worship differ from going to a temple?

Observation

1. Faith enters through crisis, not conviction. Across all five respondents, Guanyin worship began not from theological interest but from a moment of vulnerability — illness or life instability after marriage. This suggests that Guanyin worship functions less as a creed and more as a coping mechanism activated by life's pressure points.

2. Belief is almost universally reframed as psychological comfort rather than supernatural conviction. With the partial exception of 曾小梅 (who holds unreserved faith), respondents consistently hedge their belief with phrases like "who knows," or "it's a psychological anchor." This rhetorical pattern suggests that modern Chinese folk religion operates in a register of functional belief: you don't need to fully believe for the practice to work. The practice itself generates the comfort.

3. The concept of blessing is consistently moralized, not transactional. Nearly all respondents rejected the idea that prayer mechanically produces blessing. 贺奇 articulated this most clearly — "hugging the Buddha's feet at the last minute" doesn't work; only a life of consistent goodness earns protection. 周细兰 mentioned that blessing is the natural outgrowth of inner transformation, not a reward for ritual performance. This moralization of blessing suggests that folk religion here ethically structured.

4. Physical presence in ritual is considered non-negotiable and online worship is universally rejected. All five respondents have never participated in online Guanyin worship, and most expressed skepticism or distaste for it. 贺奇's saying is the most vivid: "if you won't even offer the bodhisattva a meal, why would she pay you any attention?" It's like if we say we have a business relationship and I ask you to do a favor for me. I have to ask you out and have a meal together to talk this favor through. The underlying logic is that ritual efficacy requires material presence: incense, offerings, the body in the temple. This presents a direct grounded answer: for this community, machines cannot replace the god-relationship, because the medium is inseparable from the message.

5. Family transmission is the primary (and increasingly fragile) reason of religious continuity. 贺奇 offered the most analytical account of this: in old Cantonese families, faith is transmitted by living it daily. Her educated daughter still believes. But once family lineage breaks, the tradition is nearly impossible to revive. The Party's atheist education has ensured this across the country. Faith is not spread through institutions or media; it passes person to person, and generation to generation within families.

6. The community is aging, and no clear succession is in sight. The youngest respondent began worship in her late twenties after marriage;most began in their forties. The oldest, 左发兰, is over eighty and she has no digital presence and no network among younger believers in her new location. This demographic pattern suggests the community may be contracting. Whether the young temple-goers in rural Jiangxi described by 曾小梅 represent genuine continuity, or festival-day curiosity, remains an open question for further research.
